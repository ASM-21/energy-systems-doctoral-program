# Program Overview

## How to navigate the curriculum

Use the [learner guide](learning-guide.md), [curriculum map](course-map.md), and [glossary](glossary.md) before selecting coursework. The map distinguishes active Year 1 courses from planned Year 2 study and optional extensions.

## Intellectual objective
Study energy infrastructure as a coupled system. Physical constraints shape market outcomes; market rules shape investment; regulation distributes costs and risks; public policy changes technology incentives; and strategic choices affect security, resilience, and industrial competitiveness.

## Core questions
1. How do electricity systems physically operate, and what constraints bind?
2. How are energy, capacity, ancillary services, and transmission valued and procured?
3. How do market design and utility regulation affect investment, innovation, and reliability?
4. How should uncertainty, externalities, and distributional effects enter decisions?
5. Which methods credibly estimate policy effects or optimize system operations?
6. How do infrastructure choices affect national security and industrial competitiveness?

## Competency outcomes
The learner should be able to interpret one-line diagrams, per-unit quantities, power-flow concepts, and distribution constraints; explain dispatch, congestion, LMP, ancillary services, and resource adequacy; trace federal/state jurisdiction; explain utility revenue requirements and rate design; formulate and analyze dispatch/optimization models; interpret matrix and network formulations, dual variables, uncertainty, and numerical limitations; conduct reproducible regression analysis and causal research design; distinguish descriptive association from identified effects; critically evaluate policy claims; and write for technical and policy audiences. Advanced mathematical maturity and graduate econometrics are developed in planned Year 2 sequences rather than assumed at entry.

## Geographic focus
The U.S. system is the default context. PJM is a recurring case for wholesale markets, transmission, resource adequacy, interconnection, and state-federal interactions. Pennsylvania provides a state-level case. Comparisons with other regions and countries should illuminate differences rather than add breadth without purpose.

## Research philosophy
The goal is not to accumulate facts. It is to ask precise questions, identify credible evidence, construct models, test claims, and communicate defensible conclusions. Select projects at the intersection of importance, data availability, methodological feasibility, and practical relevance.
