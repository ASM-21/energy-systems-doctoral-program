# Program Roadmap

## Design assumptions
3–5 hours/week (150–225 hours/year); existing graduate mechanical-engineering preparation and economics exposure; primary focus on U.S. electricity systems, with PJM and Pennsylvania recurring as applied cases.

## Year 1 — Integrated foundations
Understand the physical grid, economic logic of dispatch and investment, market/regulatory institutions, and reproducible empirical work.
- Power Systems and Electricity Infrastructure.
- Energy Economics and Market Fundamentals.
- Electricity Markets, Utility Regulation, and PJM.
- Applied Quantitative Methods for Energy Research.
- Output: documented PJM/Pennsylvania infrastructure or DER analysis with technical appendix and policy brief.

## Year 2 — Advanced analytical tools
- **Planned mathematical methods course:** matrix analysis, multivariable calculus, real-analysis foundations, differential equations, dynamical systems, probability/stochastic processes, convex analysis, duality, numerical optimization, graph theory, and stochastic/robust optimization. This is a two-semester modular sequence, not an additional full-time course.
- [Graduate econometrics and causal inference](courses/year-2/graduate-econometrics-causal-inference/README.md): potential outcomes, OLS, panel data, event studies, IV, difference-in-differences, regression discontinuity, synthetic control, survival/competing risks, and time series.
- Electricity market design: LMP, congestion, ancillary services, capacity/resource adequacy, market power, transmission planning.
- Optimization: LP, NLP, mixed-integer, stochastic optimization, unit commitment, dispatch, optimal power flow.
- Infrastructure economics: cost of capital, rate base, revenue requirements, depreciation, risk allocation, investment incentives.
- Output: reproducible empirical or optimization paper with sensitivity analysis and research presentation.

The mathematical sequence should precede or run alongside advanced optimization. It must fit within the existing 3–5 hour/week workload by displacing or slowing other course work, not by quietly increasing total hours. See [Year 2 course sequence](courses/year-2/README.md) and the [advanced mathematical methods course](courses/year-2/advanced-mathematics-energy-systems/README.md).

## Year 3 — Policy, transition, and strategic systems
- Climate economics and policy evaluation.
- Renewable integration, storage, transmission, demand response, DER aggregation.
- Federal and state energy policy; FERC and state commission institutions.
- Energy security, critical supply chains, industrial policy, national competitiveness.
- Output: comparative policy study with distributional and reliability analysis.

## Year 4+ — Specialization and original research
Select one primary and one complementary concentration:
- distribution planning, DER interconnection, and hosting capacity;
- utility regulation and grid-investment incentives;
- transmission expansion and regional market design;
- data-center load growth, reliability, and cost allocation;
- resource adequacy and decarbonization;
- energy affordability and distributional impacts;
- industrial policy, energy-intensive manufacturing, and supply chains;
- energy security and critical infrastructure resilience.

Output: substantial working paper, conference submission, public technical repository, and policy-facing summary.

## Progression gates
Move to Year 2 after demonstrating power-system representation and distribution analysis; supply/demand and dispatch; PJM market structure and regulatory institutions; reproducible regression/time-series work; and a Year 1 synthesis.

Begin a research paper only after a focused literature review and data-feasibility assessment. At each annual review, audit hours and evidence, identify weak concepts, revise scope, and update research priorities. This is an adaptive plan, not a promise of four-year mastery.
