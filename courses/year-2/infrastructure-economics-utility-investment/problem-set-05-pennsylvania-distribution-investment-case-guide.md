# Assessment Guide: Pennsylvania Distribution Investment and DER Integration Case

This guide defines what a defensible submission should demonstrate. There is no single numerical answer because the learner selects a public proceeding and must work from its actual record. A submission should not be rewarded for producing a positive NPV or recommending approval; it should be rewarded for correctly interpreting evidence and making a transparent decision under uncertainty.

## 1. Institutional map

The learner should distinguish the distribution utility's responsibility for its distribution facilities and interconnection process from PJM's wholesale market and regional transmission functions, FERC's federal jurisdiction, and the Pennsylvania PUC's state regulatory authority. The exact allocation depends on the issue and governing tariff/order. A party's participation in a working group does not confer jurisdiction.

A strong submission cites the operative statute, regulation, tariff, order, or governing agreement rather than relying on a stakeholder slide deck. It separates a proposal, settlement, commission order, and effective tariff.

## 2. Investment reconstruction

The analysis should separate:
- gross capital cost from grants, contributions, and customer-funded construction;
- capital expenditure from O&M;
- forecast cost from actual expenditure;
- proposed recovery from approved recovery;
- in-service plant from construction work in progress;
- accounting depreciation from economic useful life;
- project-level cost from total program or portfolio cost.

A simplified annual revenue requirement can be used only when its inputs are documented. The learner should show rate base, depreciation, allowed return, tax treatment, and O&M as separate elements. If the public record omits cost or rate-base data, the correct response is to identify the gap, not invent a proxy and present it as fact.

## 3. Cost allocation and customer impacts

A strong submission tests whether the selected allocator is linked to the cost driver. Account count may be relevant for customer-related costs; demand and coincident peaks may be relevant for capacity; energy may be relevant for throughput or losses; location and reliability may be important for targeted facilities. A single allocator should not be assumed to explain all distribution costs.

Bill impacts require the approved or proposed tariff determinants and customer usage data. Class-average effects should not be presented as customer-level predictions. Distributional analysis should consider usage heterogeneity, customer class, location, and relevant affordability measures where reliable and lawfully available.

## 4. Engineering need

The record must connect the proposed investment to an engineering problem and identify alternatives. Aggregate interconnection counts may motivate investigation, but do not establish feeder-level constraints, upgrade scope, or avoided cost. A credible engineering analysis identifies network location, loading, voltage/protection criteria, contingency assumptions, timing, and the counterfactual without the project.

The learner should explicitly distinguish what is directly measured, modeled, asserted by a party, or inferred by the learner.

## 5. Capital budgeting and risk

The analysis should avoid mixing utility cash flow, customer bill effects, and societal welfare. Rate recovery is a transfer; construction labor and materials are real resource costs. Avoided interruption damages may be benefits, but must be tied to a defensible customer interruption-cost approach and not double counted.

Risk allocation should reflect controllability and information. Procurement and project execution risks may be more controllable by management; policy, load, and external technology risks may be shared. The recommendation should consider incentives for efficient project scope, lifecycle cost, service quality, and timely delivery.

## 6. Recommendation quality

The memo should state the decision, evidence supporting it, strongest counterargument, conditions or safeguards, and unresolved facts. An evidence-insufficient conclusion can be stronger than a confident recommendation built on missing cost or engineering inputs.

## Common failure modes

- Treating a settlement proposal as an operative order.
- Treating a tariff sheet filed by a utility as effective without checking approval and effective date.
- Treating a PUC annual interconnection count as evidence of a particular feeder constraint.
- Equating proposed project cost with approved rate base or annual revenue requirement.
- Treating allowed return as a project-level cash benefit or a social benefit.
- Counting the same reliability benefit as both customer savings and societal benefit.
- Reporting class-average bills as individual customer forecasts.
- Ignoring alternatives, asset life, utilization, cost overruns, or post-investment performance.
- Using confidential employer information or implying access to nonpublic planning data.

## Grading standard

The rubric in the assignment should be applied to the reasoning and source traceability, not the direction of the recommendation. A technically sound submission may recommend approval, modification, deferral, rejection, or an evidence-insufficient finding, provided it explains the decision and its limitations.
