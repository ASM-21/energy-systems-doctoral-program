# Solution Notes: Regulatory Lag, Incentives, and Performance Regulation

All values below are in millions of nominal dollars. The examples isolate operating costs only. They are not a full revenue requirement, earnings forecast, or tariff calculation.

## Problem 1 — Static rates and regulatory lag

The allowed operating-cost allowance remains $100m.

| Year | Cost without initiative | Allowance less cost | Cost with initiative (including implementation) | Allowance less cost | Incremental initiative result vs no initiative |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 104.00 | -4.00 | 101.00 | -1.00 | +3.00 |
| 2 | 108.16 | -8.16 | 103.16 | -3.16 | +5.00 |
| 3 | 112.49 | -12.49 | 107.49 | -7.49 | +5.00 |

The initiative reduces operating costs by $5m in each year, but its $2m implementation expense reduces Year 1 net savings to $3m. Under the stylized static allowance, the utility's operating-cost position improves by those net savings relative to the no-initiative counterfactual.

The difference between allowance and operating cost is not net income. It excludes all other revenues, expenses, capital costs, taxes, depreciation, financing, regulatory assets/liabilities, and accounting or regulatory adjustments. It should not be described as the utility's total profit or shareholder return.

Regulatory lag can allow a utility to retain some benefits of cost reductions until rates are reset, but also exposes it to costs rising faster than the allowance. Whether the utility actually retains savings depends on the rate plan, trackers, sharing mechanisms, prudence review, and other adjustments.

## Problem 2 — Annual cost-of-service reset

| Year | Cost without initiative / reset allowance | Cost with initiative / reset allowance | Reduction in allowed operating-cost requirement |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 104.00 | 101.00 | 3.00 |
| 2 | 108.16 | 103.16 | 5.00 |
| 3 | 112.49 | 107.49 | 5.00 |

Under this stylized annual reset, the lower actual cost is reflected immediately in the next authorized operating-cost allowance. The customer-side reduction equals net savings. The utility does not retain a continuing operating-cost margin from the initiative under the assumed full pass-through.

Real proceedings use test years, forecasts, normalization, prudence standards, accounting rules, and evidentiary review. They do not simply reimburse every dollar spent. Costs may be disallowed, adjusted, deferred, or recovered through separate mechanisms. The timing of the reset also matters: an annual filing may still involve regulatory lag.

## Problem 3 — Earnings sharing

Net verified savings relative to the no-initiative case are $3m in Year 1 and $5m in Years 2 and 3.

| Year | Net savings | Customer 50% | Utility 50% |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 3.00 | 1.50 | 1.50 |
| 2 | 5.00 | 2.50 | 2.50 |
| 3 | 5.00 | 2.50 | 2.50 |
| **Total** | **13.00** | **6.50** | **6.50** |

Over three years, the initiative produces $13m of net operating savings under the stipulated benchmark. A 50/50 sharing mechanism allocates $6.5m to customers and $6.5m to the utility. Under the static-rate case, the utility's operating-cost position improves by the full $13m relative to the no-initiative counterfactual during the plan, subject to all omitted factors. Under the annual-reset case, the $13m is passed through to customers in the simplified allowance.

The result depends critically on the benchmark. If the benchmark is overstated, apparent savings may be inflated; if understated, the utility may have little incentive to outperform it. Thresholds, deadbands, service-quality adjustments, auditability, and the treatment of implementation costs affect the allocation. If capital expenditure receives more favorable treatment than operating expense, the utility may have incentives to capitalize costs or substitute capital for labor. Regulators need consistent accounting definitions and scrutiny of total lifecycle cost.

## Problem 4 — Mechanism distinctions

- **Cost-of-service with fixed rates:** Rates are based on an approved test-year revenue requirement and remain fixed until changed or adjusted. Shareholders may bear cost deviations between cases, subject to trackers and other provisions. Long lag can encourage cost control but can also create financial pressure or defer necessary maintenance.
- **Formula rate / annual true-up:** A defined formula updates selected revenue-requirement components. It can reduce timing mismatch and improve cost recovery, but weakens the period during which savings are retained and requires transparent inputs, prudence review, and audit controls.
- **Fuel/purchased-power adjustment:** Passes through specified volatile commodity or procurement costs, often subject to reconciliation or prudence review. It reallocates some commodity-price risk to customers and can weaken procurement incentives if not designed with benchmarks and oversight.
- **Revenue decoupling:** Adjusts rates or collections so that authorized distribution revenue is less dependent on sales volume. It can reduce the utility's disincentive toward efficiency or distributed generation, but can shift volume risk to customers and does not automatically resolve cost overruns or affordability.
- **Earnings sharing:** Shares earnings or verified savings above/below defined thresholds. It can moderate extremes but may weaken incentives if the utility retains too little of marginal savings or if benchmarks are poorly specified.
- **Performance-based regulation:** Links financial rewards or penalties to specified outputs or outcomes. It can align incentives with reliability, safety, customer service, and efficiency, but poorly chosen metrics invite gaming and may fail to capture unmeasured dimensions.
- **Multi-year rate plan:** Holds selected rates or revenue allowances stable over a defined period, potentially with inflation/productivity adjustments and an efficiency carryover. It can create cost-control incentives and predictability, but forecast error and service-quality deterioration require safeguards.

## Problem 5 — Output quality and performance

A lower expense number does not establish productivity if service output or quality deteriorates. At minimum, assess:
- Reliability: SAIDI (customer interruption minutes per customer-year) and SAIFI (interruptions per customer-year), with consistent major-event treatment and customer population.
- Safety: employee and public safety incidents per defined exposure denominator, with severity and reporting rules.
- Customer service: complaint rate per 1,000 customers and time to resolve, with consistent case definitions.
- Affordability: representative and distributional bill burden, arrears, disconnections, and payment-plan outcomes, with privacy-protective aggregation.
- Cost efficiency: controllable O&M per customer, per circuit-mile, or per standardized workload, adjusted for output, geography, weather, and service territory complexity.
- Asset health: overdue maintenance, equipment failure rates, and condition-based risk indicators.

Potential gaming includes reclassifying outages, excluding difficult-to-serve customers, deferring maintenance beyond the measurement horizon, shifting O&M into capital accounts, and reducing complaint counts by changing intake definitions. A scorecard should use stable definitions, independent audits, multi-year trends, and counter-metrics. Deadbands reduce noise-driven rewards; caps and collars limit extreme transfers; service-quality penalties protect customers; audit rights validate data. These features do not eliminate the need to assess whether the chosen metrics reflect actual public outcomes.

An output is directly measured service performance. A welfare outcome such as interruption cost requires customer damage estimates, critical-load differences, duration/timing, and willingness-to-pay or avoided-cost assumptions. It is more economically meaningful but harder to measure and more uncertain.

## Problem 6 — Memo framework

A strong memo should not select a mechanism before identifying the utility's actual risk drivers and the commission's statutory objectives. It should explain why a three-year plan is appropriate, which costs are controllable, which are volatile or externally driven, and how performance will be measured. A defensible design may combine a multi-year base-rate path for controllable costs, narrowly defined trackers for demonstrably exogenous costs, an independently audited productivity factor, and a balanced service-quality scorecard. Revenue decoupling should be considered only if sales-volume incentives are a documented policy concern and the mechanism's bill and risk effects are modeled.

The memo should explain how savings are retained and for how long, how forecast errors are handled, how cost shifts between O&M and capital are detected, and how the plan treats exceptional events. It should require transparent data definitions, public reporting, independent audit, customer bill-impact analysis, and a mid-plan review. Any proposal should be tested against historical utility data and alternative assumptions before adoption.
