# Year 2 — Advanced Analytical Methods

**Status:** Planned sequence. Do not begin until the Year 1 progression gate is met.  
**Purpose:** Move from competent use of energy models toward independent mathematical formulation, empirical identification, optimization, and institutional analysis.  
**Workload constraint:** The entire program remains 3–5 hours per week. Year 2 is a sequence of courses taken in blocks, not four simultaneous courses.

## What Year 2 is

Year 2 is a flexible advanced-study stage, not a requirement to finish every course in a single calendar year. The current course architecture represents approximately 195–250 hours before a research capstone, or roughly 9–19 months at 3–5 hours per week. Allow another 25–40 hours for a substantial research synthesis; the full stage can therefore take approximately 10–22 months, depending on prerequisite remediation, pace, and project scope.

The learner should choose a primary analytical pathway based on the research question. Do not treat every topic in the repository as a required assignment.

## Recommended order

1. **Complete the Year 1 progression gate.** Confirm that power-system, economics, institutional, and introductory quantitative foundations are secure.
2. **Begin Y2-01 mathematics.** Complete the prerequisite diagnostic and study the core sequence through linear algebra, multivariable calculus, probability, constrained optimization, and numerical methods. Advanced topics can be deferred until they are needed.
3. **Choose a primary methods branch.**
   - Choose **Y2-02 econometrics** for empirical policy evaluation, interconnection outcomes, utility investment, or program impacts.
   - Choose **Y2-04 market design** for dispatch, wholesale markets, congestion, reserves, resource adequacy, and transmission.
4. **Add Y2-03 infrastructure economics** when the research question requires utility finance, rate design, capital recovery, or regulatory incentives. It can follow Y1-03 without waiting for the full econometrics course.
5. **Complete a research synthesis** that integrates engineering, economics, methods, and institutional evidence.

At most one course should be treated as the primary weekly workload. A second course may be used for light reading or targeted problem review, but do not stack two nominal 2–3 hour courses inside a 3–5 hour total budget.

## Course dependencies

- Y2-01 is the mathematical support for advanced optimization, stochastic planning, and network methods. Not every module is a prerequisite for every later course.
- Y2-02 depends on Y1-04 regression and measurement skills. The learner must define an estimand and identification argument before choosing an estimator.
- Y2-03 depends on Y1-02 economics and Y1-03 regulatory institutions. Its accounting models must be tied to the applicable jurisdiction and source record.
- Y2-04 depends on Y1-01 power-system representation, Y1-02 dispatch economics, and Y2-01 linear algebra/optimization. Y2-02 is complementary for empirical market evaluation, not a universal prerequisite.

## Research capstone

The [Pennsylvania distribution DER interconnection capstone](../../projects/policy-analysis/pa-distribution-interconnection-capstone/README.md) is one possible synthesis project. Its current public aggregate panel supports descriptive analysis and research-design development, not a causal evaluation of application-level outcomes. The capstone should not assume access to confidential employer information or nonpublic utility records.

A Year 2 research product should include:
- a narrow question and literature positioning;
- a conceptual and mathematical or empirical framework;
- an explicit data and source register;
- documented assumptions and institutional boundaries;
- a reproducible analysis or computational model;
- independent validation and sensitivity/uncertainty analysis;
- an interpretation for a technical or policy audience;
- limitations, robustness checks, and a reproducibility package.

## Progression standard

Advance to original research only when the learner can explain the method, derive or justify its central assumptions, reproduce its implementation, and identify the conditions under which the result would not be credible. Completion of a course guide or automated test suite is not proof of mastery.
